# Peer Federation

## Definition

Peer federation is an architectural principle for decentralized, place-based governance in which autonomous nodes — communities, commons, watersheds — coordinate horizontally without ceding decision-making authority to a central control point. Rather than each community feeding into a shared central repository, peers federate directly with one another, linking "circle to circle, commons to commons, watershed to watershed." Each node remains functionally sovereign because the network architecture itself never requires authority to flow upward.

## Technical Architecture

In Benjamin Life's model (developed through [[concepts/Knowledge Commons]] work), peer federation runs on distributed repositories whose agents federate peer-to-peer instead of meta-repos that aggregate everything centrally. The mechanism rests on meta-indexing rather than centralized storage, agent subscriptions to relevant knowledge sources, and "bilateral negotiation" between schemas — where agents use bridge YAML files to translate between differing taxonomies without imposing universal standardization. After receiving a notification, each agent autonomously decides whether new content fits its own repository. This is the difference between a network that demands one shared ontology and one where, for example, "backyard gardens" in a Front Range taxonomy and "urban agriculture" in a Cascadia taxonomy can be reconciled locally without either side surrendering its language.

## Sovereignty Preserved Through Relationship

Peer federation is Benjamin's answer to the question of how to scale while preserving relational legitimacy. Vertical growth into abstract institutions tends to dissolve "the relational knowing that makes solidarity real"; horizontal federation preserves it. This ties the concept directly to [[concepts/recursive-sovereignty]] and [[concepts/subsidiarity]], where each nested level retains genuine self-governance, and to [[concepts/functional-sovereignty]], where boundaries are held by peer agreement rather than force. It also avoids the "trap of the map-maker" — the colonial pattern in which a centralized authority decides who is included and who is excluded.

## Bioregional and Polycentric Application

As substrate for [[concepts/polycentric-governance]], peer federation enables "the plurality of addressable space": multiple self-governing communities can declare their relationship to overlapping geographies — watershed councils, permaculture networks, mutual aid groups — on credibly neutral infrastructure without competing for jurisdictional control, "expressing different dimensions of relationship to the same living place." Knowledge commoning across bioregions then flows through [[concepts/Knowledge Organizational Infrastructure]], a mycelial plumbing model in which information "naturally moves through the network the way nutrients move through mycelium, finding its way to where it's needed." Concretely, this appears in the Front Range × Cascadia pilot (agents for the Salish Sea and Cowichan Valley federating patterns while keeping local autonomy), in Regen Network and Symbiocene Labs federating a bioregional knowledge commons through stigmergic signals rather than mandates, and in OpenCivics' meta-index approach to open protocol libraries. Through [[concepts/cosmolocalism]], global knowledge flows arise from a network of locally rooted, ecologically grounded communities that self-organize through permissionless, overlapping polities.

## In Benjamin's Work

Peer federation is articulated most directly in [[sovereignty-is-solidarity]] as the means of scaling without losing relational legitimacy, and in [[the-infrastructure-of-belonging]] as the substrate for place-based polycentric governance. It is also load-bearing for his [[concepts/regenerative-accelerationism]], enabling the "recursive, self-reinforcing dynamics" by which each community's capacity-building makes it easier for the next to follow — without centralized permission-granting or gatekeeping.
