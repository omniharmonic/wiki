# Capitalist Realism

Capitalist realism is the term coined by the cultural theorist Mark Fisher for "the widespread sense that capitalism is not only the only viable political and economic system, but also that it is now impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it." It is the mature, exhausted condition of a culture that has lost the capacity to picture any outside, summed up in the line Fisher borrowed from Fredric Jameson and Slavoj Žižek: that "we can now sooner imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism."

The concept describes the experiential texture of a [[saturation|saturated]] system. When the [[egregore|egregore]] of capital has metabolized every position outside itself, opposition does not disappear, it is digested and sold back: the revolutionary becomes a face on a T-shirt, the punk scene a clothing line, the critique of consumer culture an advertisement. What remains is not triumphant ideology but a flat, depressive horizonlessness, a foreclosure of [[political-imagination|political imagination]] that Fisher tied directly to rising mental ill-health under neoliberalism. It is the felt underside of the [[end-of-history|end of history]].

Capitalist realism marks the limit that pure critique cannot cross. Postmodern deconstruction can name the cage with exquisite precision, but it cannot open the door; held alone, clear sight curdles into the quiet nihilism that is the real mood of the moment. Escaping capitalist realism is therefore not a matter of better argument within the old grammar but of a [[evolutionary-phase-change|phase change]] to a new one, crystallizing a livable [[the-ancient-future|alternative]] rather than merely critiquing the existing order.
