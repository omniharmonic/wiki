# Imaginal Cells

Imaginal cells are the cells inside a chrysalis that carry the pattern of the butterfly the caterpillar is becoming. The metaphor, drawn into cultural-evolutionary discourse by biologist Bruce Lipton and others, rests on a striking fact of metamorphosis: the caterpillar does not sprout wings, it _dissolves_, its body breaking down into an undifferentiated cellular soup. Floating in that soup are the imaginal cells, which carry the blueprint of a creature that does not yet exist. At first they appear in ones and twos, isolated, and the dissolving body's immune system does not know what to make of them. Then they begin to find one another, linking and resonating until they reach a critical coherence, past which the whole soup reorganizes around them.

As a model of transformation the image carries three lessons. First, the new form is built not by defeating the old body but by cohering inside its dissolution, so collapse and renewal are intertwined. Second, the reorganization happens not by majority vote or force but by _recruitment_: a coherent pattern, once it catches, draws everything around it into its order. Third, what looks at first like an isolated anomaly, easily dismissed, is in fact the seed of the next order. The butterfly is not a better caterpillar; it is a different grammar of what that life can be.

Imaginal cells are the biological cousin of Prigogine's [[islands-of-coherence|islands of coherence]] and supercooled water crystallizing around a speck of dust, all describing how systems far from equilibrium reorganize through [[dissipative-structures|dissipative]] coherence rather than argument. As social strategy the metaphor counsels those who would seed a [[evolutionary-phase-change|phase change]]: not to win the old order's argument but to embody a coherent fragment of the new world, become an island of coherence, and find the others, a practice resonant with the [[the-great-turning|Great Turning]] and [[emergent-strategy|emergent strategy]].
