# Dissipative Structures

Dissipative structures are ordered systems that arise and sustain themselves _far from thermodynamic equilibrium_ by continuously dissipating energy. The concept earned the chemist Ilya Prigogine the 1977 Nobel Prize and overturned a common intuition: a system pushed far enough from equilibrium does not collapse into formless chaos and stay there. Past a critical threshold it spontaneously reorganizes, discovering, out of its own turbulence, a new and higher order. The title of the book Prigogine wrote with the philosopher Isabelle Stengers gives the principle its perfect name: _Order Out of Chaos_.

The significance reaches beyond chemistry. Prigogine was a creature of the modern grammar, a hard physical scientist, and yet what his science reached at its own frontier was a cosmos that _creates itself_, that is in some irreducible sense alive. Even physics, followed all the way down, began to leak out of the machine of dead matter. Dissipative structures supply the rigorous, non-mystical basis for claiming that collapse and creation are not opposites: the breakdown of an old order at the edge of dissolution is precisely the condition under which a new one can emerge.

This makes dissipative structures the scientific spine of [[evolutionary-phase-change|evolutionary phase change]]. When a system [[saturation|saturates]] and is driven far from equilibrium, it reorganizes around [[islands-of-coherence|small islands of coherence]], local pockets of a new pattern that, once coherent enough, recruit the whole. The same dynamic appears in Bruce Lipton's [[imaginal-cells|imaginal cells]], in supercooled water crystallizing around a speck of dust, and in Margaret Wheatley's account of social emergence, all of them instances of a [[complex-adaptive-systems|complex system]] finding a new [[basin-of-attraction|attractor]] under stress.
