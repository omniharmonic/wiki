# Cascadia Bioregional Movement

## Definition

The Cascadia Bioregional Movement is a 40+ year-old socio-political project that organizes governance and culture around watershed boundaries rather than colonial political lines. Originating with David Henke and other early bioregional leaders in the 1980s–90s bioregional congresses, it is embodied in Cascadia today by practitioners and organizations practicing watershed-based governance, post-ideological coordination, and cosmolocal knowledge sharing. In Benjamin's framing, it is a pragmatic response to institutional collapse — not utopian fantasy — and the only viable vision that takes seriously both global coordination needs and the primacy of local relationship while remaining achievable now.

## Origins and Praxis

The movement traces to the 1980s–90s bioregional congresses, which Benjamin describes as "laboratories for reimagining democracy from the ground up" — grounded in watersheds rather than the arbitrary political lines inherited from colonial history. Its contemporary praxis emphasizes watershed-based governance, post-ideological coordination rooted in material interdependence, and [[wiki/concepts/cosmolocalism|cosmolocal]] knowledge sharing: global knowledge flowing freely while implementation stays local.

The underlying logic is forced solidarity: you cannot exit a watershed. Because everyone sharing a watershed is materially bound to one another, that shared dependence creates the conditions for coordination across otherwise irreconcilable ideological divides.

## Practitioners and Institutional Self-Negation

In Cascadia specifically, the movement is carried by practitioners like Darren Zal (working on a Salish Sea knowledge commons) and Shawn Anderson (Cowichan Valley, Front Range × Cascadia bioregional pilot), alongside organizations such as Regenerate Cascadia. These organizations practice what Benjamin calls "institutional self-negation" — maintaining legal nonprofit status while refusing permanent authority, treating themselves as temporary scaffolding for network self-organization rather than as durable centers of power.

This connects the movement to a broader practice of restoring civic culture through the daily discipline of shared stewardship — embedded in every organization's decision-making rather than confined to annual congresses.
