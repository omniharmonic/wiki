# Punctuated Equilibrium

Punctuated equilibrium is the theory, proposed by paleontologists Stephen Jay Gould and Niles Eldredge in 1972, that evolutionary change is not the smooth, gradual ascent of the textbooks but a rhythm of long stasis broken by sudden, structural leaps. Species persist relatively unchanged for vast stretches, then transform rapidly in geologically brief bursts, often associated with speciation events. Read across the fossil record, the pattern is one of equilibrium "punctuated" by abrupt reorganization.

Its importance here is as independent evidence for the same rhythm that recurs across complexity science and cultural evolution. The geological record, the chemistry of [[dissipative-structures|dissipative structures]], the metamorphosis of [[imaginal-cells|imaginal cells]], and the crystallization of supercooled water all tell one story: systems do not only change by smooth increments; they accumulate tension within a stable form until a threshold is crossed and the whole configuration leaps. Long stasis is not stagnation but the [[saturation|saturation]] phase, the working-out of every move a given form allows.

For civilization, punctuated equilibrium underwrites the claim that the present convergence of crises will not resolve through another incremental turn of the existing [[dialectics|dialectic]] but through an [[evolutionary-phase-change|evolutionary phase change]], a punctuation that installs a new grammar. The leaps are sudden in hindsight precisely because they are structural: they redefine the possibility space rather than rearranging its contents.
