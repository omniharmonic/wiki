# From Moral Authority to Parity

"From moral authority to parity" names the transition strategy by which a parallel cooperative economy can outcompete an extractive one that is vastly subsidized — by externalized ecological costs, by monetary policy, by captured regulation. Evaluated purely on sticker price today, a federated cooperative food chain cannot beat a big-box grocer whose true costs are billed to the atmosphere, the aquifer, and the future. So the competition does not begin on price.

It begins on *legitimacy*: on moral authority, on the relational pull of an economy in which people can feel that their spending builds their neighbors' wealth rather than a distant portfolio. Households with the surplus to do so migrate their spending for reasons of meaning and membership before reasons of margin. That migration is the ignition.

Then the terms of competition themselves shift. Prices in the regenerative economy decrease through closed loops, mutualistic financing, and shared infrastructure, while extraction becomes more expensive as its externalities come home — uninsurable regions, snapped supply chains, volatility that reprices everything. The cooperative economy's structural advantages compound: economies of scale that federate without remitting profit to shareholders, obligation-clearing among enterprises that trade with each other, a workforce that owns its work. Parity arrives when these two trajectories intersect and the regenerative economy becomes *the* economy — not through good will, and not because the cooperative economy learned to extract, but because extraction's mounting costs finally exceed its benefits. Past parity lies the upward spiral: circulation compounds, surplus feeds solidarity funds, solidarity funds establish new cooperatives. The whole economy need not convert. One closed loop in one bioregion whose demonstrated prosperity becomes the template is enough. This is the economic expression of [[dual-power|dual power]] and [[parallel-construction|parallel construction]].
